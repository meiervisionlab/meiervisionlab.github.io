### what does the lab do?

This is a basic science lab focused on sensation, perception, and visual neuroscience. While our research may have translational relevance, we are not involved in clinical practice or patient care. Student roles center on computer-based experiments with human participants, using tools such as eye tracking, EEG, and psychophysical methods.

You might enjoy working in our lab if you've ever wondered to yourself: Why do illusions work even when you know they're not real? How do we integrate signals across all of our senses to form a coherent experience? In what ways does the brain act like a filter, emphasizing some inputs while ignoring others? How can we measure what someone sees or feels using only brain or eye signals? What do models of vision reveal about how the brain encodes the world? You should be comfortable with thinking about the brain as an information-processing system—how signals are encoded, transformed, and interpreted to produce perception and behavior.

### undergraduate student opportunities

  * We welcome motivated undergraduate students to inquire about research opportunities in the lab. Our work is well-suited for students planning to pursue graduate training in psychology, neuroscience, vision science, or related disciplines—as well as those interested in gaining scientific research experience before applying to health professions or computational fields. 
  
  * If you're interested in joining the lab, please email Dr. Meier with a brief statement describing your academic background, relevant coursework or skills, and what draws you to the lab's research. Include your resume or CV and your unofficial transcript.


### graduate student opportunities

  *  **Current students** interested in working/collaborating with us should send an email or drop by my office (JDA 2147) -- if the door's open, I'm happy to chat.

  *  **Prospective students**: Graduate students (MS, OD/MS, and PhD) are admitted through [the UHCO's Graduate Program in Physiological Optics/Vision Science](https://www.opt.uh.edu/education/graduate-programs/index.cfm). Applications are typically due end of January. You are welcome to get in touch around October through the end of January to discuss graduate school fit. Strong candidates are curious, analytical, and motivated to understand how the brain constructs perception from sensory signals. You should be comfortable with data and eager to learn experimental and computational tools. Students from neuroscience, psychology, cognitive science, and related domains tend to be a strong match. Clinically-trained applicants (e.g. ODs) who are motivated by basic science questions and comfortable thinking about perception in computational terms are also welcome.
